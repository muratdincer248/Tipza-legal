Almost every restaurant has a tip rule. Far fewer have it written down, and the difference between the two only shows up at the worst possible moment: a disputed payout, a resignation, a new manager who divides Saturday differently because nobody told them otherwise.

The fix is smaller than it sounds. Not a contract, not an appendix to the staff handbook — one page that answers six questions, dated, where people can see it.

<AnswerBlock>
  A tip policy should answer six questions on a single page: what counts as a
  tip, who shares in it, how it is divided, when it is paid, who does and checks
  the arithmetic, and how the policy changes. Date every version, agree it with
  the team before it takes effect, and keep the superseded versions. Length is
  not the measure of a good policy; being read is.
</AnswerBlock>

## Why the written version behaves differently

An unwritten rule is not a weaker version of a written one. It is a different thing, because it has no fixed content — it is whatever the person explaining it remembers, and that changes with who is asked.

<PullQuote>
  A rule survives staff turnover. A habit leaves with the person who was keeping
  it.
</PullQuote>

Writing it down does three specific things. It fixes the content, so two people cannot both be right about what the rule is. It makes the rule reviewable, so it can be argued with in advance rather than during a payout. And it makes it inheritable, so the next person to close the till is applying the same rule rather than reconstructing one.

## The six questions

### 1. What counts as a tip

The boundary case is the one that causes trouble, so name it explicitly. Cash left on the table, an amount added on a card terminal, a rounding-up, a service charge printed on the bill, a payment through an app — these do not all behave the same way, and in several countries they are not even the same thing legally.

Write which of them go into the pool and which do not. If a service charge is treated as revenue rather than as a tip, say so here, in one sentence, rather than letting people discover it from a payslip.

### 2. Who shares in it

List the roles, not the people. "Service, bar, runners and kitchen" ages well; a list of names needs editing every time someone is hired.

Then answer the two questions the list will immediately raise: what happens to a trial shift or an agency worker, and whether salaried supervisors are included. Both are things somebody will ask in the first month.

### 3. How it is divided

The method itself, with the numbers. Not "fairly, according to contribution" — that phrasing looks reasonable and settles nothing. If it is hours-weighted, say so and give the formula. If it is points, publish the point values in the policy, because a weighting that lives in someone's spreadsheet is not a rule anyone can check.

<TipBox title="Include one worked example">
  A single fully worked shift does more than three paragraphs of description. It
  removes the ambiguity about rounding, about whether breaks count, and about
  what happens to the odd cents — all the things a method statement leaves open
  and every real payout has to resolve.
</TipBox>

### 4. When it is paid

Per shift in cash, weekly, or with the monthly payroll. Give the actual timing, and say what happens when it falls on a holiday.

This line looks administrative and prevents a surprising amount of friction, because "when do I get it" is the question people are actually asking when they ask how tips work.

### 5. Who does the arithmetic, and who checks it

Name the role that performs the calculation and the role that can verify it. The second half is the one usually left out, and it is what turns the policy from a description into something with an appeal route.

Add where the record lives — the app, the shared folder, the binder behind the pass — so checking does not require asking permission.

### 6. How the policy changes

State what triggers a review and who has to agree a change. Triggers work better than dates: a new role, a change in headcount, a menu change, a shift in how guests pay.

Naming this in the policy is what stops mid-argument renegotiation. When someone wants to reopen the point values after a bad Saturday, the answer is already written down, and it is a process rather than a refusal.

## Where it is a legal duty

In the United Kingdom this stopped being optional. Under the Employment (Allocation of Tips) Act 2023, an employer at whose place of business tips are paid on more than an occasional and exceptional basis must have a written tipping policy and make it available to all workers<SourceRef sources={frontmatter.sources} id="uk-tipping-act" />. The statutory code of practice sets out what the policy should cover — how tips are accepted, how they are allocated and distributed, and the steps taken to handle them fairly and transparently<SourceRef sources={frontmatter.sources} id="uk-tipping-code" />.

The record-keeping duty is the part that catches people out. Employers must record every qualifying tip and its allocation, and keep that record for three years from the date the tip was paid. Workers can request it — once per three-month period, covering up to three years back<SourceRef sources={frontmatter.sources} id="uk-tipping-act" />.

The Employment Rights Act 2025 adds consultation and review on top: consulting worker or union representatives before the first version of the policy, reviewing it at least every three years with consultation each time, and making an anonymised summary of the consultation available to everyone at that place of business<SourceRef sources={frontmatter.sources} id="uk-era-2025" />. Those provisions commence by regulation rather than automatically, so check the current commencement position before relying on a particular date.

<Callout title="Outside the UK, the incentive is different but it points the same way">
  Germany, Austria and most of Europe impose no written-policy duty. What they
  do have is a tax authority that will ask how a pooled arrangement works. In
  Germany the exemption in § 3 Nr. 51 EStG turns on the money coming from a
  guest and carrying no legal
  claim<SourceRef sources={frontmatter.sources} id="estg-3-51" /> — questions
  that are much easier to answer with a dated document than with a recollection.
  A written rule is not required. It is simply the artefact that makes the
  conversation short.
</Callout>

## Three ways a policy fails

**It is too long to be read.** Four pages of careful drafting produces a document nobody consults, which leaves you with a habit and a false sense of coverage. Cut to a page. If the rule will not fit, simplify the rule.

**It describes an intention rather than a procedure.** "Tips are shared fairly among the team in recognition of everyone's contribution" survives every review and answers no question anyone has. If a sentence cannot produce a number, it is decoration.

**It is never updated.** A policy that still describes a two-person kitchen and mostly-cash payment is worse than none, because people stop trusting the parts that are still accurate. This is what the review trigger in question six is for.

<WarningBox title="Do not write in a discretion you do not intend to exercise">
  "Management reserves the right to vary the distribution" is a common last line
  and a genuinely bad one. It tells the reader that everything above it is
  provisional, which removes the reason the document existed — and in
  jurisdictions where tip treatment depends on the employer not controlling the
  entitlement, a broad discretion can affect more than morale.
</WarningBox>

## The version that gets used

Aim for something that would not impress a lawyer: one laminated sheet by the pass, six short sections, a worked example from an ordinary Friday, a date in the corner.

A document like that is argued over exactly once, by the people it applies to, and then settles every payout after that. Which is the entire job.
