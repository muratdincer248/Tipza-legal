There are two kinds of advice about earning more tips. One kind is a colleague's theory, delivered with total confidence at 1 a.m. The other kind has been tested on real tables by researchers with clipboards, most famously at Cornell, where Michael Lynn spent decades measuring what actually moves the number on the check<SourceRef sources={frontmatter.sources} id="megatips-2" />.

The good news: the tested list is short, cheap, and none of it requires becoming a different person. The better news: most of it is just being a person, on purpose.

<AnswerBlock>
  Make a small, genuine connection at every table: introduce yourself by name,
  get to eye level, repeat the order back, and write a short thank-you on the
  check. Each of these has been field-tested and shown to raise tips. Guests
  tip a person they connected with more generously than a service they
  consumed.
</AnswerBlock>

## Guests tip people, not service

Almost every technique that survives testing works the same way: it turns you from an order-carrying process into a specific human being. People are more generous to someone they feel connected to — that is the whole trick, and it cannot be faked by volume.

The tested versions of "be a person"<SourceRef sources={frontmatter.sources} id="megatips-2" />:

- **Introduce yourself by name.** A name makes the tip personal. Stiffing "the service" is easy; stiffing *Alex* is slightly harder, and slightly is where tips live.
- **Get to eye level.** Crouching or sitting at the table for the order — where the room allows it — reliably raised tips in experiments. Looming is for lighthouses.
- **Smile like you mean it.** Guests can tell the difference, which is why the fake version does not test well and the real one does.
- **Repeat the order back.** It signals you listened *and* catches the mistake before the kitchen commits to it. Double win, zero cost.

## The check is your last word

The tip is decided in the final ten seconds, staring at a piece of paper or a terminal screen. The research is clear about what to do with that moment: personalise it. A handwritten "thank you" on the check — ideally with your name — is one of the most consistently replicated tip-raisers ever tested<SourceRef sources={frontmatter.sources} id="megatips-2" />.

Some of the tested variants get more creative: drawing a smiley face, forecasting good weather for tomorrow, a sweet with the bill. You do not need to become a weather service. You need the last impression to come from you and not from the card machine.

<TipBox title="Bartenders and baristas: same trick, shorter window">
  No table, no check to write on — but the mechanism travels. Remember the
  regular's order. Use the name on the cup or the card. Be visibly unhurried
  for the ten seconds you are with them, even when you are hurried everywhere
  else. Connection compresses fine.
</TipBox>

## Raise the bill honestly

Most guests tip roughly in proportion to the total, which means the most boring lever is also the strongest: a bigger bill at the same percentage is a bigger tip. The tested route is suggestive selling — a genuinely good recommendation, made once<SourceRef sources={frontmatter.sources} id="megatips-2" />.

"Genuinely" is load-bearing. A recommendation the guest is happy about raises the bill *and* the percentage. A pushed dessert nobody wanted also shows up in the tip, just with the sign flipped.

## What the research does not say

Worth knowing before you rearrange your personality around a bibliography:

- **The effects are real but not magic.** A statistical review of the whole literature concluded most published techniques would replicate — and also that the average effect is a nudge, not a jackpot<SourceRef sources={frontmatter.sources} id="p-curve-tips" />.
- **Most experiments are American.** Where tipping is smaller and more habitual — much of Europe — the same techniques move a smaller number.
- **Nothing survives bad basics.** No amount of check-doodling rescues a wrong order or cold food. Charm on top of a mistake reads as a distraction from it.

<WarningBox title="Run your own experiment, gently">
  Even Lynn's advice to servers is to test techniques on your own shifts,
  because rooms differ. Try one change for a week — the name, the thank-you,
  the eye level — and watch your own average. Your section is a better lab
  than someone else's restaurant.
</WarningBox>
