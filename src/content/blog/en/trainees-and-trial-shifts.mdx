Every restaurant that hires gets this wrong at least once. A new server shadows for a week, watches tips get divided at the end of every shift, and nobody mentions whether they are in the pool or not. By the time someone asks, the answer is already late — and whatever it is, it feels like it was made up on the spot.

The experienced staff feel protective. They covered the section, answered the trainee's questions, and caught the mistakes. Splitting equally from Day 1 ignores that contribution. But telling a new hire who ran plates for six hours that they receive nothing is the sort of policy that reaches a review site before it reaches management.

There is a middle path, and it is not a compromise — it is a structure.

<AnswerBlock>
  Use a graduated entry: zero points while shadowing, a reduced share while
  supervised on the floor, and full points after probation. Write the stages
  into the tip policy before the first trainee arrives, so the rule is an
  onboarding step and not an improvisation.
</AnswerBlock>

## The legal starting point

Before the fairness question comes the ownership question: whose money is it?

In Germany, a tip handed directly by a guest to an individual belongs to that individual<SourceRef sources={frontmatter.sources} id="gewo-107" />. This applies regardless of contract status. A trainee on a paid working trial (Probearbeit) has an employment relationship and should be covered by the house tip rule. A person on a short unpaid observation (Einfühlungsverhältnis) does not — but if a guest puts money into their hand, that money is still theirs.

Pooled tips are different. When the team collects tips into a shared pot and divides them by a rule, inclusion in the pool is a matter of agreement, not automatic entitlement. That is why the written policy matters: it is the document that says who is in, from when, and at what share.

<Callout title="The UK position since the 2023 Act">
  Under the UK Employment (Allocation of Tips) Act, all workers at a place of
  business are covered — and the statutory code specifically lists agency workers
  and those on variable contracts among those who should be considered for fair
  allocation<SourceRef sources={frontmatter.sources} id="uk-tipping-code" />.
  Excluding a trial-shift worker who provided service is harder to defend under
  this framework, though the code allows the allocation to reflect factors like
  role, seniority and hours.
</Callout>

## Three stages, one progression

The model below is not the only way, but it is simple enough to explain on someone's first morning and fair enough that nobody has resigned over it.

The numbers above use a points system, but the principle works with any method. In an hours-weighted split, the equivalent is adjusting the hours multiplier: 0 during shadow, 0.5 during supervised, 1.0 at full status. The logic is the same — a graduated contribution earns a graduated share.

## Worked example

A Thursday evening shift with a pool of €480 and the following crew:

David's 0.5 points produce a share that is roughly half of what he would have received at full weighting. That is visible in the breakdown, explainable to David, and defensible to Anna and Ben — who carried more of the section and received more of the pool.

When David moves to Stage 3, his points change to 1.0 and the next split adjusts automatically. No ceremony, no negotiation, just a number on his profile.

  cleared. The roster remembers the value between splits, so the progression
  does not need re-entering every night.

## Trial shifts: the ethical line

A trial shift sits in an uncomfortable gap. It is short enough that many businesses treat it as informal — and informal enough that the tip question gets skipped entirely.

Here is the practical test: did the person serve guests? If they carried plates, made drinks, cleared tables or took orders, they contributed to the service that generated the tips. Whether you owe them a share depends on the jurisdiction and the arrangement. Whether you should give them one depends on what you want the next candidate to say about working here.

<WarningBox title="The reputational cost is asymmetric">
  A generous trial-shift policy costs a few euros per candidate. A story about
  a venue that kept a trial worker's tips costs interviews, because the story
  will be told by every person who hears it — including the ones you want to
  hire next.
</WarningBox>

A reasonable default: if the trial is a pure observation with no customer-facing work, the person is not in the pool. If it is a working trial where they contributed to service, they enter at the Stage 2 rate (0.5 points or equivalent), with that expectation set in writing before the shift starts.

## Writing the trainee rule into the policy

The trainee rule is not a separate document. It is a paragraph in the house tip policy — ideally in the section that answers "who is included".

What it needs to say:

1. **The stages and their point values** — shadow at 0, supervised at 0.5, solo at 1.0 (or whatever the team agreed).
2. **Who decides the transition** — typically the floor manager or head server, based on operational readiness.
3. **A maximum duration for each stage** — so that half-points do not become permanent by inertia.
4. **The trial-shift rule** — whether and at what rate a trial participant enters the pool.

Write it before the first trainee arrives. A rule introduced after someone is already on the floor at 0.5 points looks like it was built around a specific person, however fair the number actually is.

## What goes wrong without a written rule

Three failure modes, all avoidable:

**The manager improvises.** Every trainee gets a different deal depending on who was closing, what the mood was, and whether someone remembered to ask. Inconsistency is the fastest way to make a fair method look arbitrary.

**The trainee finds out late.** Nobody mentioned tips during onboarding, and the trainee assumed they were included. The conversation at the end of Week 2 is now a confrontation instead of an explanation.

**The half-point stage never ends.** A trainee who is clearly running a solo section still has 0.5 points because nobody updated the record. This is not a policy failure — it is a data hygiene issue, and it is exactly the kind of thing a scheduled review catches.
