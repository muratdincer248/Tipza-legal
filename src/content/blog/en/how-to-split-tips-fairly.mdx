Most tip disputes are not really about money. They are about a number nobody can explain. When a server finishes a busy Saturday and receives less than the colleague who left three hours early, the problem is not the amount — it is that no one can say *why*.

A tip splitting method is the explanation. Pick one, write it down, apply it the same way every shift, and the arguments mostly stop. What follows are the five methods teams actually use, what each one rewards, and where each one falls apart.

## Start by deciding what you are rewarding

Every method encodes a belief about what earns a share of the tips:

- **Presence** — being there for the shift at all.
- **Time** — the number of hours actually worked.
- **Role** — the responsibility and skill the position demands.
- **Sales** — the revenue an individual personally brought in.

None of these is objectively correct. A small café where everyone does everything has a different answer than a fine-dining room with a sommelier, three servers and a runner. The methods below are just different weightings of the same four ideas.

## 1. The equal split

Total the tips, divide by the number of people who worked the shift.

> A team of four collects €240 in tips. Each person receives €60.

**It suits** small teams, shifts of roughly equal length, and rooms where everyone genuinely covers for everyone else.

**It breaks down** the moment shift lengths diverge. Somebody who worked three hours takes the same share as somebody who worked nine, and the nine-hour person notices immediately. It is the simplest method to calculate and the fastest to feel unfair.

## 2. The hours-weighted split

Divide the pool in proportion to hours worked. Calculate a per-hour rate, then multiply by each person's hours.

> €240 in tips across a shift with 4 + 8 + 6 + 6 = 24 hours worked.
> The rate is €240 ÷ 24 = €10 per hour.
> Shares: €40, €80, €60 and €60.

**It suits** almost every team with variable shift lengths, which is most teams. It is the default for a reason: hours are already tracked, the arithmetic is checkable by anyone, and the result matches most people's intuition about fairness.

**It breaks down** when roles differ sharply in responsibility. An hour of running plates and an hour of managing a section of twelve tables count identically, which experienced staff tend to push back on.

## 3. The points (role-weighted) split

Assign each role a point value, multiply points by hours, then divide the pool by total points.

| Role | Points per hour |
| --- | --- |
| Server | 1.0 |
| Bartender | 1.0 |
| Runner | 0.7 |
| Host | 0.5 |
| Kitchen | 0.5 |

> €240 in tips. A server works 8 hours (8.0 points), a bartender 6 hours (6.0), a runner 6 hours (4.2) and a host 4 hours (2.0) — 20.2 points in total.
> The rate is €240 ÷ 20.2 = €11.88 per point.
> Shares: €95.05, €71.29, €49.90 and €23.76.

**It suits** larger teams with clearly distinct roles, and any operation that shares tips with the kitchen.

**It breaks down** on the politics of the point values themselves. The numbers are a judgement about relative worth, and every review of them is a negotiation. Set them once, with the team, and revisit them on a schedule rather than after a bad shift.

## 4. Percentage of personal sales

Each person keeps a percentage of the tips generated on their own checks, often with a fixed cut passed to support roles.

> A server closes €1,800 in sales, receives €190 in tips on those checks, and passes 20% — €38 — to the support pool. They keep €152.

**It suits** rooms where servers own their sections end to end and where the point of sale can attribute every check reliably.

**It breaks down** where sections are not equal. The terrace on a warm evening and the corner by the kitchen door are not the same job, so the method quietly rewards the seating plan as much as the service. It also discourages helping a colleague who is drowning, which is expensive in ways that do not show up in the split.

## 5. The hybrid: pooled front, fixed share back

Pool all tips from the front of house, distribute that pool by hours or points, and pass a fixed percentage of the total to the kitchen.

> €240 in tips, with 25% (€60) to the kitchen.
> The remaining €180 is split across 24 front-of-house hours at €7.50 per hour.

**It suits** operations where the food is a large part of why people tip, and where the kitchen would otherwise be excluded.

**It breaks down** if the fixed percentage is never revisited. A number set when the menu was six dishes rarely still fits a kitchen twice the size.

## Choosing between them

A short version, for teams that want to stop deliberating and start:

- Shifts all roughly the same length, three or four people: **equal split**.
- Variable shift lengths, one broad role: **hours-weighted**.
- Distinct roles, or tips shared with the kitchen: **points**.
- Servers own their sections and the point of sale is reliable: **percentage of sales**.
- Front and back both need a defensible share: **hybrid**.

## The rule matters more than the method

Whichever method you choose, three things do more for fairness than the choice itself.

**Write it down.** A method that lives only in the head of whoever closes the till is not a rule, it is a habit, and it will drift.

**Apply it the same way every time.** One exception, made for good reasons on a difficult night, becomes the precedent for the next argument.

**Show the working.** People accept a smaller share far more readily when they can see the arithmetic that produced it. This is the whole reason Tipza shows every step of a split rather than just the final numbers — a share nobody can check is a share somebody will eventually contest.

One practical note before you change anything: how tips are pooled can affect how they are taxed, and the answer differs by country. In Germany, tips handed directly to an employee are treated differently from money the employer collects and redistributes. If you are moving to a pooled model, read up on the tax treatment before the first payout rather than after it.
